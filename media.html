Outage shows how users need to look for alternatives, expert says (opens in a new tab)

ABC News reported my interview on the ABC News Channel in its live coverage of the global Microsoft outage. I said it was too early to judge how big the outage could become, and that a failure of this kind would have been very difficult to avoid. Cloud services remain valuable, but reliance on one underlying technology is their downside. Organisations and individuals should identify the essential services that must keep running, arrange alternatives from a different provider, and test them, because an untested plan is likely to fail during a real outage.

The Conversation

Microsoft has suffered a global outage. A tech expert explains what went wrong (opens in a new tab)

A global Microsoft outage stopped email, cloud storage and collaboration tools for many users, with effects that reached family violence services, schools, telehealth and government service centres. It even disrupted AI tools that connect to Microsoft 365. In this article for The Conversation I explain why Microsoft's account of the failure shifted and why the evidence does not suggest a cyberattack. A more mundane explanation, in which faulty health checks withdraw healthy servers, fits better, but it remains an inference until a post-incident review is published. I argue that individuals and organisations should prepare for digital outages as they do for bushfires, with offline essentials and backup plans that are tested in practice.

The Conversation

What would happen if the internet went down? (opens in a new tab)

A complete internet blackout is unlikely, because the network is built from many cables, wireless links and satellites that can route around failures. The more realistic risk is that many services fail together because they depend on the same shared system, such as a single sign-in provider. A recent flaw in PHP showed how one vulnerability can take many websites offline, and AI tools are making such flaws easier to exploit. In this article for The Conversation I describe what that could look like, from a cyberattack on health systems that delays insurance claims while clinics keep seeing patients, to email, documents and learning platforms failing at the same time. I also set out how organisations can prepare with a tested incident response plan, and how households can keep essential information offline, know alternative ways to sign in, communicate and pay, and test their backups. I discussed these questions in a radio interview with ABC Southern Queensland, which is featured on this page.

AI research tools can distort findings and misattribute authors, CQUniversity researcher warns (opens in a new tab)

Researchers increasingly ask AI tools to find and summarise the literature, and the answers read as authoritative. Tests of those summaries found missing or misattributed authors, incomplete references and inaccurate accounts of what the studies actually found. The practical lesson is that a fluent summary is not evidence that a paper says what the summary claims, so every claim that matters must be checked against the original source. This is the same issue that SAGE-R addresses for graduate researchers, who must understand and verify a claim before relying on it. The story was published by CQUniversity News.

Students are asking for AI guidance, not just policy: a comprehensive, practical framework for AI-enabled assessment (opens in a new tab)

Universities have responded to generative AI mainly through policy and integrity warnings. These are necessary, but students are asking for something more practical: how to verify AI output, how to write a good prompt and how to avoid breaching the rules by accident. This article, published in HERDSA Connect, draws on research with first-year ICT students at CQUniversity. It shows that when students are given structured verification requirements, they check AI output systematically and revise it substantially instead of making cosmetic edits. It also notes that confidence in English is a major reason students turn to AI, so a blanket ban can remove a useful support for linguistically diverse students. The article sets out the SAGE workflow for assessments where AI use is permitted, with a decision log and a critique stage, and suggests that educators start small with a permitted-use statement and rubric criteria that reward the quality of justification.

Smart cities of the future could think for themselves (opens in a new tab)

Today's smart cities collect data for people to interpret. In my book, Next-Generation Smart Cities, I argue that the next step is cities in which networks of autonomous AI agents reason, act and coordinate with each other in real time. Traffic is a simple example: instead of reporting congestion to a control room, intersections could coordinate directly with buses, ambulances and neighbouring roads. The book also examines how blockchain and future 6G networks could help protect connected infrastructure. The harder questions concern accountability. If an autonomous system makes a faulty decision in a hospital, a transport network or a power grid, it is not clear who is responsible, and trust, safety and ethical design must be settled before these systems become embedded. CQUniversity News published this feature on the book.

Times Higher Education — THE Campus

Students are asking for AI guidance, not just policy (opens in a new tab)

Co-authored with Professor Ergun Gide for Times Higher Education Campus, this piece argues that policies, warnings and bans are not enough. A policy sets boundaries but does not teach students how to verify AI output, and it overlooks the way AI can help students from linguistically diverse backgrounds take part more fully. We present SAGE as a practical alternative: students generate a response, evaluate it against authoritative sources, refine it with a logged decision to accept, modify or reject, use AI as a critic, reflect, and defend their work in a short supervised task. We also offer a seven-step guide for applying the approach to any unit, including weighting assessment towards process rather than the final product, and we recommend that educators begin with a permitted-use statement, a simple decision log and rubrics that judge the quality of justification.

Future Campus

AI Era Must Not Become Excuse to Default to Low Tech Exams (opens in a new tab)

Many universities are reacting to generative AI by returning to invigilated exams, handwritten tasks and oral checks. In this opinion piece for Future Campus, I argue that this overcorrects. Exams often reward recall under pressure, can disadvantage students with anxiety or language barriers, and rarely resemble how graduates work. Because graduates will use AI and remain accountable for its output, assessment should test their evaluative judgement in AI-rich settings. I propose distributed assurance: rather than one secure format for every unit, each subject includes supervised checkpoints suited to its discipline, such as a viva, a code walkthrough or a live demonstration. SAGE puts this into practice through its final Defend step, in which students show ownership of their reasoning.

Teaching students to think with AI — not cheat with it (opens in a new tab)

Universities cannot reliably detect AI use, and banning it is unrealistic when employers expect graduates to work with it. In this opinion article, CQUniversity researchers argue that the real question is whether students understand what they submit. The answer offered is SAGE, a six-step learning cycle: students generate a draft with AI, check it against professional standards, correct its errors, have the AI critique the revised work, reflect on where the tool succeeded or failed, and defend their competence in a short supervised task. The article also points to the regulator's recommendation to combine open assessments that build AI skills with secure assessments that verify independent knowledge. Published on elkhodr.com

Preparing the next generation of researchers to work with AI

A candidate who begins a PhD this year will graduate around 2030, in a world where AI is part of everyday research practice. The question for graduate research is therefore no longer whether candidates will use AI, but whether they will be equipped to use it well. Guardrails tell candidates what is permitted, but they do not show how to do sound research alongside AI. In this article I argue that the difference lies in what AI does to a research claim. Most assistance, such as finding papers, fixing code or tidying prose, leaves the claim unchanged. Sometimes, however, AI shapes the claim itself, and SAGE-R calls that moment the ownership threshold. SAGE-R is the research supervision extension of SAGE that I developed with Professor Ergun Gide. It gives candidates and supervisors a shared way to check, decide and explain what they rely on, and every resource is open.

Published on elkhodr.com

What the Binance hack reveals about blockchain security

When billions of dollars leave a cryptocurrency exchange, it is fair to ask what happened to the security that blockchain was supposed to provide. I teach blockchain at CQUniversity, and this article uses the Binance incident to answer that question in plain terms. A blockchain protects the shared record of transactions, but an exchange holds its customers' keys and approves transfers through its own internal systems. On the evidence reported so far, attackers appear to have compromised those systems and caused them to authorise payments that carried genuine signatures, so the blockchain itself was not broken. The lesson is that a decentralised network can still depend on one centralised approval process, which then becomes a single point of failure. I explain what a wallet actually holds, how a transfer is authorised and the controls that could contain the damage, themes that are developed further in my book on smart cities.

Protect your research in the AI era

What is shared with an AI tool may leave the author's control, and an unpublished idea that is retained or used in training could resurface in someone else's answer, uncredited. This poster sets out six checks to make before sharing: whether the contribution is worth protecting, whether the material is sensitive, whether policy and permission allow the use, how the service trains on and retains inputs, how far an AI agent should be fenced in, and whether a brief, dated description would serve instead. It was prepared with Professor Ergun Gide for Cyber Security Awareness Month and first appeared in CQUniversity's Researcher Essentials in October 2026.

The SAGE Framework for Developing Critical Thinking and Responsible Generative AI Use in Cybersecurity Education (opens in a new tab)

The SAGE paper in Discover Education is indexed in ERIC, the education research database of the United States Department of Education's Institute of Education Sciences. The paper, written with Professor Ergun Gide, introduces SAGE, a framework that uses generative AI responsibly to build critical thinking in cybersecurity education. Students first use AI in tutorials to produce, evaluate and improve security policies, and then apply AI outputs to realistic industry cases in assessments. The authors report improved ability to assess policies and refine risk assessments, alongside over-reliance on AI and uneven AI literacy, and they stress the need for human oversight.

European Parliament analysis cites research on identifying fake news in the Russian-Ukrainian conflict (opens in a new tab)

A European Parliament in-depth analysis of the use of artificial intelligence as a weapon of repression, requested by the Parliament's Subcommittee on Human Rights and published in May 2024, is listed in SciVal as citing a 2023 conference paper on identifying fake news in the Russian-Ukrainian conflict using machine learning. The paper, led by Omar Darwish with Dr Mahmoud Elkhodr among the co-authors, appeared in Lecture Notes in Networks and Systems from AINA 2023. It compared a pre-trained BERT model with five classical classifiers on a labelled dataset of tweets, and found that support vector machines and logistic regression performed best, at about 76 per cent accuracy. The analysis was also issued by the EU Publications Office.
